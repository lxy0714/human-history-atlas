This page exists because a history product that will not say where its content came from is worse than useless. Read it before you cite anything.

## 1. Provenance of the content — the honest version

**The 2.61-million-character corpus in this repository is AI-assisted writing.**

That sentence is the most important thing on this page, so here is exactly what it means and what it does not mean.

### What it means

- The prose was generated by a large language model, working from established mainstream historical consensus, and organised into a typed schema by the project author.
- Selection, emphasis, framing, wording and the choice of which nodes deserve long-form treatment are **machine-generated decisions**, reviewed by a human only partially.
- English translations, where they exist, are also machine-generated and follow the same review status.

### What it does not mean

- It is **not** a transcription, excerpt, translation or paraphrase of any single reference work. No book was digitised into this repository.
- It is **not** a scrape of Wikipedia or any other encyclopedia.
- It is **not** peer-reviewed scholarship.
- It is **not** a primary source for anything.

### The consequence you must accept

> **Do not cite this corpus as an authority.** Not in an essay, not in a paper, not in journalism, not in a classroom assessment, not in a legal filing.

Factual claims follow mainstream consensus, and the great majority of nodes will be broadly correct. But *broadly correct* is not *verified*, and a corpus of 763 nodes at this length will contain errors — of date, of attribution, of emphasis, and of omission. Some are certainly still present. Nobody has read all 2.61 million characters against sources.

**Finding and reporting those errors is the single most valuable contribution you can make to this project.** See [Contributing](CONTRIBUTING.md).

## 2. What the content rests on

The model's historical knowledge derives from the general historiographical consensus represented in its training data — the standard academic and popular synthesis of world history. In practice this means the content aligns with the kind of framing you would find in:

- general world-history surveys used in undergraduate teaching;
- standard periodisations (Prehistoric / Ancient / Classical / Medieval / Early Modern / Industrial / Modern);
- mainstream archaeological and chronometric dating for prehistoric and ancient nodes;
- conventional dynastic chronologies for Chinese history;
- widely accepted dates and attributions for events, persons and artefacts.

**This is a description of alignment, not a bibliography.** No specific title in that list was consulted, copied, or verified against during the writing of any particular node. Claiming otherwise would be exactly the dishonesty this page exists to avoid.

### Known systematic biases to watch for

| Bias | Where it shows | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronological uncertainty flattened** | Prehistoric and early Bronze Age dates presented as single years when scholarship gives ranges | Treat any pre-1000 BCE year as approximate |
| **Eurocentric periodisation** | "Medieval" applied globally, though it is a European period | The era bands are a navigational convenience, not a claim about world periodisation |
| **Consensus lag** | Recent archaeological revisions may not be reflected | Verify anything contested |
| **Confident tone on contested questions** | The prose does not reliably hedge where scholars disagree | Cross-check contested attributions |
| **Uneven depth** | 270 Chinese nodes vs 15 Oceanic nodes | Depth reflects source availability, not historical importance |

## 3. Editorial decisions that *were* deliberate

These are human choices, and they are defensible:

1. **Ten civilisations, not five.** Africa (46), Central Asia (23), the Americas (50) and Oceania (15) were specifically expanded during development. A timeline that skips them is not a human timeline. This was a correction applied after the first build, not an original virtue.
2. **Every node gets a body.** All 763 nodes have long-form prose — no stubs, no "coming soon". This was enforced by a test.
3. **No orphan nodes.** 76 isolated nodes were found and connected; the integrity test now forbids regressions. A history graph where things do not connect teaches the wrong lesson.
4. **2,396 unique relation pairs**, deduplicated and bidirectional, with zero dangling references and zero self-loops — all test-enforced.
5. **576 nodes flagged `important`** drive the projector's event stream. This is an editorial judgement about what belongs in a 3-million-year overview, and it is arguable. Argue with it in an issue.
6. **No emoji, no gamified confetti.** The tone is a museum, not a mobile game.

## 4. Design inspiration

The visual and interaction language borrows from specific traditions:

### Museum vitrines and exhibit labels
The default theme is a dark wood display case with warm gold hairlines. Node cards are shaped like **exhibit labels**: square corners (2px), thin rules, dense small type, left-aligned. The 2px corner radius is enforced at build time by a PostCSS plugin precisely so this identity cannot drift.

### Thread-bound Chinese books (线装书)
The vertical reading mode is right-to-left, top-to-bottom, with a serif CJK face — the layout of a classical thread-bound volume, not a CSS rotation trick. The **bamboo** theme extends this to the colour of inked bamboo slips.

### Xuan paper and ink
Two themes (**xuanzhi**, **ink**) come from the paper-and-ink tradition: one plain white for long reading, one OLED black for night. **Parchment** is the Western counterpart. Five themes exist because reading environments differ, not because theme pickers are fashionable.

### Satellite archaeology
The map uses **Esri World Imagery** rather than a political map. This is a deliberate historical choice: political borders are anachronistic for almost every node in the corpus. Rivers, coastlines, mountain passes and deserts are what actually constrained human history, and satellite imagery shows them without imposing a modern nation-state layer on the Bronze Age.

### Timeline tools and grand-strategy games
The swimlane view and the History Projector owe a debt to two traditions: academic parallel-timeline charts, and the era-progress interfaces of grand-strategy games. The projector's year cursor sweeping from 3,000,000 BCE is unabashedly the latter — watching time move is a genuinely effective way to feel scale.

## 5. Technical inspiration and dependencies

The engineering stands on open-source work. Full licence details are in [Third-Party Notices](THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.md).

| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| **React 19** + **Vite 8** | Application and build |
| **TypeScript 5.9** | Content-as-code; the schema *is* the editorial contract |
| **Tailwind CSS 4** + **Radix UI** | Styling primitives and accessible menu/dialog behaviour |
| **Leaflet 1.9** | Map interaction |
| **ECharts 5.6** | Charts in the special halls |
| **Framer Motion 12** | Transitions (deliberately excluded from the projector's event stream) |
| **Vitest 5** | 154 unit tests + 17 build gates |
| **lucide-react** | Icons |

Two ideas worth naming because they shaped the project more than any library:

- **Content-as-code.** Storing 2.61 million characters as typed TypeScript rather than in a database means every factual correction is a reviewable diff, every relation is type-checked, and the whole atlas is a static artefact anyone can host. It trades editorial convenience for auditability. For a history project, that is the right trade.
- **Gates over intentions.** Every performance and integrity property this project claims is enforced by a test that fails the build. A first-screen budget nobody enforces is a wish. This discipline came from repeatedly discovering that a claimed fix had not actually happened.

## 6. What is *not* ours

- **Satellite tiles** — Esri. Not covered by this repository's licences.
- **Fonts** — ZCOOL XiaoWei, Noto Sans SC, Noto Serif SC, served by Google Fonts.
- **Historical facts** — nobody owns these. The CC BY-SA licence covers *our prose*, not the events it describes.

## 7. How to correct something

1. Open an issue using the **Factual correction** template.
2. State the node id, the current text, the corrected text, and **a source**.
3. A source means a scholarly work, a primary document, an archaeological report, or an institutional publication — not another encyclopedia, and not another AI.
4. Corrections with sources get merged. Corrections without them get discussed.

The project would rather be corrected than defended.
